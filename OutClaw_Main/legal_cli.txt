**ISSUE**                            The central issue is whether a charging affidavit for domestic battery, signed by officers who did not witness the alleged incident, affects the validity of the complaint under state evidence rules, constitutes Brady material, or renders the charging instrument void *ab initio*, particularly when the defendant claims self-defense under the Castle Doctrine.

**RULE**

1.  **Probable Cause for Charging Instruments:**
    *   The Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution requires that an arrest warrant or charging instrument (such as an information or complaint) be supported by probable cause.           *   **_Beck v. Ohio_**, 379 U.S. 89, 91 (1964): "Probable cause exists where the facts and circumstances within the officers' knowledge and of which they have reasonably trustworthy information are sufficient in themselves to warrant a man of reasonable caution in the belief that an offense has been or is being committed."
    *   **_Illinois v. Gates_**, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983): "Probable cause can be based on hearsay, as long as the magistrate has a substantial basis for crediting the hearsay." This means an officer can rely on information provided by victims, witnesses, or other officers, even if they did not personally observe the crime.        
2.  **Castle Doctrine (General Principle):**
    *   Many U.S. states have adopted some form of the "Castle Doctrine," which generally provides that an individual has no duty to retreat and may use deadly force to protect themselves or others against an intruder or attacker within their home if they reasonably believe such force is necessary to prevent death or great bodily harm. Specifics vary by state.
                                     3.  **_Brady v. Maryland_ (Exculpatory Evidence):**                           *   **_Brady v. Maryland_**, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963): The prosecution has a constitutional duty to disclose evidence favorable to the accused that is "material either to guilt or to punishment."
    *   **_United States v. Bagley_**, 473 U.S. 667, 682 (1985): Evidence is "material" if "there is a reasonable probability that, had the evidence been disclosed to the defense, the result of the proceeding would have been different." This includes evidence that could be used to impeach a prosecution witness.

4.  **Void *Ab Initio*:**
    *   A legal instrument is considered "void *ab initio*" (Latin for "void from the beginning") if it was invalid from the moment it was created, having no legal force or effect whatsoever. This typically applies to instruments that lack fundamental requirements like jurisdiction, proper authority, or are based on fraud that vitiates the entire proceeding.

**ANALYSIS**

1.  **Validity of Complaint Signed by Non-Witness Officers:**
    *   Under established U.S. criminal procedure, an officer does not need to be an eyewitness to the crime to sign a charging affidavit or complaint. The purpose of such an instrument is to establish probable cause for the arrest or charge, not to provide direct trial testimony. Officers routinely sign affidavits based on reliable hearsay, such as statements from the alleged victim, other witnesses, or the results of their investigation (e.g., physical evidence, recorded statements).
    *   Therefore, the mere fact that the two officers who signed the charging affidavit did not personally witness the incident **does not, by itself, invalidate the complaint or render it void under state evidence rules related to probable cause.** The relevant inquiry is whether the information *contained within* the affidavit, regardless of its source, establishes sufficient probable cause to believe domestic battery occurred.              *   *Counterargument:* If the affidavit *falsely stated* that the officers witnessed the incident, or if the officers *knowingly or recklessly included false information* or *omitted material exculpatory information* that they knew to be true, this could potentially lead to a challenge under **_Franks v. Delaware_**, 438 U.S. 154 (1978). A successful *Franks* challenge could result in the suppression of evidence or even dismissal of charges if, after excising the false statements, the affidavit no longer establishes probable cause. However, the user's facts only state they "did not witness," not that they made false claims about witnessing.
                                     2.  **Brady Material:**
    *   The simple fact that the officers who signed the affidavit were not eyewitnesses is **not, on its own, _Brady_ material.** This is standard operating procedure for many arrests and charges. It is not inherently exculpatory, nor does it necessarily impeach the credibility of the alleged victim or other witnesses.
    *   *However,* specific information *related to* the officers' non-witness status *could* become Brady material if it reveals exculpatory evidence. For example:
        *   If the officers' reports or internal communications reveal that their investigation uncovered evidence supporting the defendant's self-defense claim (e.g., the alleged victim admitted to being the aggressor, or physical evidence contradicted the victim's account), and this information was *not* included in the affidavit or provided to the defense, then *that specific information* would be Brady material.
        *   If the officers' lack of direct observation meant they failed to document crucial exculpatory details that a witness might have observed, and their internal notes or interviews reflect this oversight or the exculpatory details, then those details would be Brady.
    *   The mere fact they weren't witnesses doesn't make their non-observation exculpatory; what they *did* or *didn't* observe, and what they *knew* or *didn't know* as a result, could be.
                                     3.  **Void *Ab Initio*:**
    *   A charging instrument is rendered void *ab initio* only in extreme circumstances, such as a complete lack of jurisdiction by the court, a fundamental constitutional defect that cannot be cured, or the absence of *any* factual basis for probable cause on the face of the instrument.
    *   As established above, an affidavit signed by non-witness officers, relying on credible hearsay, is a common and legally permissible practice for establishing probable cause. Therefore, the situation described **would not typically render the charging instrument void *ab initio*.**
    *   A successful *Franks* challenge, while potentially leading to dismissal, usually does not retroactively make the *original* filing "void *ab initio*" unless the entire basis was fraudulent from the outset.

**CONCLUSION**
                                     The charging instrument is likely **not void *ab initio***, and the fact that the two officers who signed the affidavit did not witness the incident is **not, in itself, Brady material** or a basis to invalidate the complaint under state evidence rules.

*   **Reason:** The use of non-witness officers to sign affidavits based on probable cause derived from hearsay is standard and accepted legal practice in U.S. criminal procedure. The bar for voiding an instrument *ab initio* or for triggering Brady disclosure is much higher. However, the specific *content* of the officers' investigation and affidavit could lead to challenges, which is why the confidence isn't absolute "High."
